# Talk track · the bucket, the backyard, and the questions

*For Kyle at a table, a meeting or a park bench. Read it twice, then put it away: your own words beat these.*

## How the table does the hard part

You said you'd rather not talk to strangers but can put the mask on, like bartending. So let the table open every conversation for you:

- **The sign speaks first.** "Ask me what your block remembers." People who stop have already chosen to talk.
- **The QR is the exit for shy people.** "Rather not chat? Scan it." Nobody feels trapped, so more people stop.
- **The tablet does the middle.** Once their address is typed, the screen is the topic, not you.
- **The bucket is the show.** One pour answers "how does it work?" better than a minute of words.
- **Count conversations, not sales.** Two hours, then stop. Ten good conversations is a win.

## Openers (pick one, say it like a bartender)

- "Want to see what your block remembers? It's free, takes two minutes."
- "Do you know your flood zone? It's one letter. Want to see the rest of the story?"
- "Thinking about buying, or just curious about your street?"

## The 30-second version

> "Your home inspector checks the house. Nobody checks the block. Flood, heat, the ground and wind don't stop at the property line, and the public records about them are scattered across a dozen agency websites. We put them on one page, free, with the source on every line. If you want more, we run our own flood, heat and ground models around one house."

## The bucket demo (about 45 seconds)

Hold up a cup of water over the bucket. Pour it slowly while you talk.

> "This is physics. Water goes downhill. It soaks in where the soil lets it, and it slows down where the ground is rough.
>
> Physics is formulas, and we know these formulas well. So we give a computer the shape of the ground, down to about a meter. How rough the streets and yards are. What's paved and what's soil, and how fast each soil drains. Then we give it a storm, and gravity does the rest. That's a simulation.
>
> It's not a crystal ball. It shows what happened in the storms we ran, and every result says how much evidence stands behind it. Today that mostly says *exploratory*, and we print that word big. But it's a lot better than one letter on a flood map."

What changed from your draft, and why: "we know the formulas, so we know the answers" becomes "so we can run the storm". The model's answer is only as good as its inputs, and the claims bar on the site says we never promise an outcome. The honest version also lands better: people trust someone who names their limits.

## Your backyard story (about 45 seconds)

> "I grew up with the catch basin in our backyard. Then the neighbor raised their yard to level it out, and all their water ended up at one spot: ours. Our backyard was flooded all the time. My dad was handy enough to put in a French drain, and it fixed it.
>
> Nobody could have told us that before the neighbor did it. That's the kind of thing I want people to see coming. So the house report has a what-if tool: add a French drain, a rain garden or a rain barrel, and watch where the water goes in our flood model. And you can read your block's record for free right now."

Hand them the French drain card at the end of this story. It's the same story on paper.

## The questions you'll get

**"Is this like First Street?" / "Is this a risk score?"**
> "They give your house a number. That's useful, but you can't check it. We show the actual records with their sources, and when we model your house we show the method and the limits. It's the difference between a grade and the graded paper."

(Don't knock them. Let the comparison do the work.)

**"Is it accurate?"**
> "Honestly? The public records are what the agencies publish. Our models are checked against published benchmark tests, and the results, including the ones that failed, are on the site. At the scale of one house nobody has measured yet, so we label our results exploratory. We'd rather you hear that from us."

**"Is this insurance? Are you selling my address?"**
> "Not insurance, not a score, not engineering. It's a screening, like a first look. We read the whole block, not just your house, and we never sell your address."

**"How much?"**
> "The block record is free, as many addresses as you want. The house report is the paid part, for one house."

(If sale is still invite-only when you're out, say "it's opening by invitation, want me to add you?" and write down name and email only if they say yes.)


**"Does it do heat?"**
> "Yes: street air temperature and how comfortable it feels outside, in sun and in shade. It's further along at night than in the afternoon. The afternoon has a lot more going on: sun, shade, reflections, trees breathing out water, the pavement itself. We're working on it, and the report says which is which."

(Check the current heat receipts on the site before using this line. The air temperature, the surface temperature of the pavement and how it feels (PET) are three different things: keep them apart.)

**"What about wind?"**
> "Not yet. Our wind model has to pass its tests first, and it hasn't. When it does, it joins the report."

**"I'm not buying a house."**
> "Then it's just a fun look at your street. Look up where you grew up. Everybody does."

## The close

> "Here's a card. Scan it whenever, type any address, and you'll see what your block remembers."

Then stop talking. Let them go. A good close leaves people wanting to look, not feeling sold.

## Words to leave at home

Safe · predict · accurate (about a result) · will flood · likely · risk score (ours) · any dollar loss or premium · hot-day counts. If you catch yourself saying one, say "well, what our model showed in the storms we ran is...". People respect the correction.
